Automated weekly pricing quality monitoring across 42 countries

Catching mismatches between a CRM and multiple ERP systems that nobody was checking.

Context

A global science and technology company sets customer-specific prices across 42 countries and about 400K products per legal entity. Each agreement is entered in Salesforce and then loaded into several ERP systems, manually or automatically. Three regional pricing teams (about 20 people) own these conditions.

Problem

An internal audit flagged that nobody monitored the quality of pricing conditions in the ERP. No one could confirm that agreements had been loaded correctly and as approved. Both manual and automatic loading can go wrong, and mistakes were only caught if someone noticed a discrepancy on an order, by which time a customer might already have paid the wrong price.

What I built

An automated weekly report that compares each week's pricing agreements in Salesforce with the ERP, field by field: dates, material, discount and discount type, currency, customer numbers and more. It classifies each difference by type (for example, a wrong discount type, a mismatched end date, a product missing in the ERP, or an agreement already superseded by a newer one) and shows them by legal entity, so each team sees what to fix. Alerts go to the pricing teams so they can correct errors right away.

Behind it is a pipeline that cleans and standardizes the Salesforce and ERP sources separately, runs dedicated matching steps for different pricing types, and combines everything into one mismatch table. The data came from 8 datasets and amounted to hundreds of millions of rows, which I reduced to a weekly view.

My role

I did the exploratory analysis, data cleaning, pipeline design and build, and the dashboard myself. The pricing teams helped me diagnose a few mismatches I couldn't explain on my own.

Results

  • Moved pricing quality from no checking at all to an automated weekly check across all regions, live since September 2026
  • Around 5% of checked agreements showed mismatches that previously would have gone unnoticed
  • The pricing teams address every flagged mismatch within the week
  • Resolved the audit finding
  • All three regional teams use it independently of me, and more are being onboarded
“Thank you for producing and rolling out the [Salesforce–ERP] comparison tool. This is answering a requirement of the audit, but also addressing a real business need.”Senior colleague, Global Pricing
“Your work will make management of [pricing agreement] data quality much more efficient and help to resolve an audit issue.”Head of Global Pricing

Hardest part

Standardizing across regions and systems. Pricing differs by region (customer number types, material codes, which pricing types are used, different Salesforce processes), and the data lived in several different ERPs with different structures. The challenge was building one standardized report that every team could use, with no separate version per region or system.

Approach

I built the logic in plain Python (PySpark) in an iterative notebook environment so I could refine the matching step by step. I chose one global report over per-region versions to keep it maintainable.

What this looks like in a smaller company

Smaller companies often have the same hidden risk: prices, orders or customer records in a CRM that don't match their accounting or ERP system, and nobody checking. The logic is plain Python, the dashboard can be built in whatever software you use, and I only need the raw data. I can set up the same weekly reconciliation with alerts for you.

Tools: Python, PySpark, Palantir Foundry (Code Workbook, Contour, Automations)

One-click ELISA analysis inside the lab notebook

Replacing a manual Excel and GraphPad workflow with a reproducible, automated pipeline.

Context

A research team at a global science and technology company tested lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) for mRNA delivery research, running ELISA assays as part of their experiments. I worked on this as a six-month, part-time data science assignment (Oct 2025 – Mar 2026).

Problem

Each experiment's results were extracted by hand, processed in Excel and GraphPad, and compiled into summary tables. The process was slow, prone to errors, and relied on each scientist knowing the right steps.

What I built

A Python pipeline that runs inside the team's electronic lab notebook. A scientist clicks one button in the experiment they're already working in, and the pipeline does the rest:

  • Pulls the raw plate data through the notebook's API
  • Fits the curves and extracts the key metrics
  • Writes the results back into the experiment as three summary tables (means, standard deviations, errors, concentration averages, etc.) and a bar chart comparing the formulations

It shows a clear error message when a step fails and says why. I containerized it with Docker so it deploys the same way every time. It's built for the team's standard ELISA plate layout, so other assay formats would need new analysis logic.

My role

I wrote the whole pipeline myself: the API integration, the analysis logic, the output formatting, containerization and deployment. The ELISA analysis was specified by the team through their existing Excel files, and I worked within the existing GitHub repository and the notebook's API.

Results

  • Replaced the manual Excel and GraphPad workflow with one-click analysis, so results are consistent and no longer depend on one person's know-how
  • Checked the outputs against completed experiments from the team's own Excel files
  • After a company-wide demo to about 100 colleagues, I received multiple messages from people across the company who wanted to use it, besides the team I was hired for
“Kalina demonstrated strong technical skills by developing software that seamlessly interacted with diverse data types and a complex infrastructure, all while adhering to best practices around code quality, user experience, and security.”Senior colleague, Data Science (Bioinformatics)

Hardest part

Getting up to speed on the notebook system and its API at the start. I had no prior experience with either and had to learn them while building.

What this looks like in a smaller company

Many companies have a recurring analysis done in a spreadsheet by one person who knows the steps. I can build the same kind of one-click automation inside the tools your team already uses, with consistent results, clear error messages, and no new software to learn.

Cutting late pricing loads from 31.6% to 0%: a process improvement project

Lean Six Sigma Blue Belt project, 2022.

The problem

A pricing team manually loaded customer-specific agreements into SAP when they couldn't transfer automatically from Salesforce. In the baseline period of 3 months, 31.58% of these agreements were loaded after their approved start date, which risked customers being charged the wrong price. The process depended on people remembering to check scattered reports, with no clear ownership and no escalation when something stalled.

What I did

I led the project from start to finish as project manager. I measured the problem and traced every late case back to its root cause. Most came from fragmented reporting, missing habits for checking, and gaps in user training. Then I:

  • Built one standardized Salesforce dashboard to replace the scattered reports
  • Redefined who handles which agreements
  • Introduced visualizations that flag incorrect submissions so they can be sent back
  • Ran knowledge-sharing sessions across 8 regions
  • Set up monthly KPIs and regular reviews so the improvement would last

Results

  • On-time loading rose from about 68% to 100% of in-scope agreements (roughly 150 a week) between 19 September and 12 October 2022, beating the 80% target in the project charter
  • The result held across the wider scope too
  • It has held since then as far as I know
  • Completed as a certified Lean Six Sigma Blue Belt project (exam plus completed project, awarded by my employer)

Skills: project management, root-cause analysis, dashboard design, stakeholder management across regions, process improvement

SaaS user retention and growth analysis

A take-home exercise on a synthetic SaaS dataset: what drives value, which onboarding approach works better, and how much capacity to plan for.

What I did

  • Used SQL for segment revenue and usage analysis
  • Compared tenure-weighted revenue across segments
  • Tested two onboarding approaches with Welch's t-test and effect size
  • Built a daily-active-user forecast validated on a 2025 holdout, with 95% prediction intervals

Findings (on synthetic data)

Usage is similar across segments, but enterprise accounts have 5–13x higher tenure-weighted MRR. Onboarding B lifts average adoption intensity by about 15% (a small-to-moderate effect). Expected Q1 2026 daily active users are around 1,620, with an upper bound near 1,950.

Predicting overweight across Dutch demographic groups, with a fairness check

MSc thesis, Tilburg University, 2025.

Approach

I compared linear regression, random forest, XGBoost and LightGBM on aggregated public CBS survey data (2016–2024). I validated the way a real forecaster would, with leave-one-year-out cross-validation plus a final test on unseen 2023–24 data.

Findings

The simplest model, linear regression, predicted overweight best, with an average error of about 2.9 percentage points on unseen years. For severe overweight, LightGBM did best, at 1.63 points. Errors were not spread evenly: mid-life adults, single households and lower-educated groups had up to four times the error of the best-served groups.

Takeaway

Start with a transparent baseline, validate across time, and check who a model serves worst.
